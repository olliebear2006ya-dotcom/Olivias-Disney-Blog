The Perfection Paradox: Why the “Zero Failure Protocol” is about People, not Code

Posted on February 16, 2026 | ENGL 170 Reflections

I recently came across Jeremy Brunt’s post on the Zero Failure Protocol, and it has been rattling around in my brain ever since.

On the surface, the title sounds like the ultimate engineer’s pipe dream: a system where failure simply doesn't happen. But as you dig into Brunt’s argument, you realize he isn’t pitching a magical silver-bullet library or a foolproof deployment script. Instead, he’s challenging the very way we conceptualize "error."

Here is my take on why the Zero Failure Protocol is a necessary—if uncomfortable—evolution for modern engineering teams.

1. Moving from "Prevention" to "Immunity"

In the traditional software world, we focus on prevention. We write tests to stop bugs from reaching production. Brunt’s thesis suggests a shift toward immunity.

"It’s the difference between a glass vase (fragile) and a living organism (resilient)."

If we accept that hardware will fail, networks will drop, and humans will make typos, then a "Zero Failure" state isn't one where those events are absent; it’s one where the system is designed to absorb those shocks without degrading the user experience.

2. The Cultural Debt

The most striking part of the "Zero Failure" philosophy isn't the technical architecture—it’s the cultural shift. Brunt touches on a reality many of us face: we often treat failures as moral failings of the developer rather than systemic gaps.

To move toward a Zero Failure Protocol, a team must have high psychological safety. If an engineer is afraid to report a "near miss" because of how it will look in a post-mortem, that hidden weakness stays in the system until it causes a catastrophic collapse. Zero failure requires total transparency, which is a people problem, not a coding problem.

3. Is "Zero" actually possible?

Critics might argue that "Zero" is an impossible (and expensive) standard. Diminishing returns usually kick in after the third or fourth nine of availability. However, I don’t think Brunt is arguing for literal 100% uptime in a vacuum.

He’s arguing for a Protocol—a set of behaviors and architectural choices that treat every failure as a bug in the design, not an inevitability of the universe. When you stop saying "failures happen" and start saying "this failure was allowed by our current design," your engineering rigor changes overnight.

4. The Constraints of Reality
